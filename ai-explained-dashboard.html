A very friendly guide

AI is just a very fast guesser.
Let's meet it.

This is a little dashboard that explains AI and Large Language Models (LLMs) using the same simple words you'd use with a five-year-old — then goes a bit deeper into real models, real companies, and real jobs AI does today.

Level 1 · The basics

What is "AI," really?

Imagine teaching a puppy tricks — except instead of a puppy, it's a computer, and instead of treats, it learns from millions and millions of examples.

🧩

AI is a pattern-finder

Artificial Intelligence is software that gets better at a task by studying lots of examples, instead of being told exact rules for every situation.

Like you're 5Show a computer 1,000 pictures of cats. It learns "cat" without anyone explaining whiskers.
🎯

It's not magic — it's math

Underneath, AI turns words, pixels, or sounds into numbers, then does enormous amounts of arithmetic to find the most likely answer.

Like you're 5It's a giant guessing machine that's really, really good at guessing because it practiced a lot.
🚫

It doesn't "know" like you do

AI has no memory of being alive, no feelings, and no understanding the way humans do — it recognizes patterns and predicts what comes next.

Like you're 5It's more like a super-fast copycat than a thinking friend.
Meet the family

AI has different "jobs" inside it

"AI" is the big umbrella word. Underneath it are different specialists.

📚

Machine Learning (ML)

The general idea of a computer improving from data, instead of from hand-written rules. Almost all modern AI is built on ML.

🖼️

Computer Vision

AI that's specialized in understanding photos and video — like recognizing faces, reading street signs, or spotting a tumor in an X-ray.

🔊

Speech & Audio AI

AI that turns spoken words into text (or text into a voice) — think of Siri, Alexa, or auto-captions on videos.

💬

Large Language Models (LLMs)

AI that specializes in language — reading, writing, and chatting. This is what powers Claude, ChatGPT, and Gemini. It's the star of this dashboard →

Level 2 · The chatty AI

What is a Large Language Model?

An LLM is a specific kind of AI that has read an enormous pile of text — books, websites, conversations — and learned how words tend to follow each other.

The cat sat on the mat

This is basically all an LLM does, over and over: look at the words so far, and guess the single most likely next word — then the next, then the next — until it's written a whole sentence, paragraph, or poem.

🍭

"Large" means huge practice

These models are trained on a giant slice of the internet, plus books and code — often hundreds of billions of words.

Like you're 5Imagine reading every book in every library, then trying to guess how any sentence ends.
🔮

It predicts, one piece at a time

LLMs don't write a whole answer at once. They generate small chunks called "tokens" (roughly word-pieces) one after another, super fast.

Like you're 5It's like finishing your friend's sentences, except it's finishing its own, forever.
🎭

It can sound sure — and still be wrong

Because it's guessing based on patterns, an LLM can confidently say something false. This is often called a "hallucination."

Like you're 5It's like a kid who tells a made-up story very confidently because it *sounds* right.
Zoom in

Two words you'll hear a lot

🪙

Tokens

The small pieces text gets chopped into — sometimes a whole word, sometimes just part of one. "Dashboard" might become "dash" + "board." Models read and write in tokens, not letters.

⚙️

Parameters

The "knobs" inside the model — numbers that were tuned during training. More parameters usually (not always) means the model can capture more complex patterns.

Level 3 · Behind the curtain

How does a model actually learn?

Think of training an LLM like teaching a kid to write — first by reading a mountain of books, then by getting feedback on their own writing.

01

Pre-training: reading everything

The model is shown huge amounts of text and repeatedly plays "guess the next word." Every wrong guess nudges its internal knobs (parameters) a tiny bit, millions of times over.

months of computing, huge cost
02

Fine-tuning: learning manners

Humans show the model examples of good, helpful answers so it learns to actually follow instructions instead of just rambling like the internet.

supervised examples
03

Reinforcement learning from feedback (RLHF)

People rate multiple answers from the model ("this one's better than that one"). The model is nudged to produce more of what people preferred — including being safe and honest.

human preference data
04

Inference: you chatting with it

This is what happens when you actually type a message — the finished model predicts tokens one by one to write a reply, using everything it "practiced" before.

this happens every message
One more idea

Context window: its short-term memory

An LLM doesn't remember you between separate chats (unless a product explicitly adds memory). Inside one conversation, it "sees" everything typed so far — up to a limit called the context window — kind of like how much a person can hold in their head at once before forgetting the beginning.

Sizing them up

"Small," "medium," "big" — why does it matter?

Most model families ship in a few sizes. It's less about which is "best" and more about the right trade-off for the job.

🐇

Small / fast models

Cheaper and quicker, great for simple tasks: quick replies, sorting, summarizing short text, or running on a phone.

Like you're 5A quick errand-runner — fast, but not who you'd ask to solve a mystery.
⚖️

Mid-size models

The everyday workhorse for most tasks — balances quality, speed, and cost for most chatbots and apps.

Like you're 5A reliable all-rounder who's good at most homework subjects.
🐘

Large / frontier models

The most capable models — best for hard reasoning, coding, research, and complex multi-step tasks, but slower and pricier.

Like you're 5The wise expert you call in for the really tricky problems.
Not all AI chats are the same

Open weights vs. closed models

🔓

Open-weight models

The company publishes the trained model file so anyone can download, run, and modify it on their own computers (e.g. Llama, Mistral, DeepSeek). Great for privacy and customization.

🔒

Closed / hosted models

The model only runs on the company's own servers, accessed through an app or API (e.g. Claude, GPT, Gemini). Usually the most capable and best-supported option.

🧱 Foundations

Artificial Intelligence (AI)+
The broad field of building machines that perform tasks normally requiring human intelligence, such as recognizing images or understanding language. The term was coined for the 1956 Dartmouth Summer Research Project by John McCarthy.Source: Dartmouth Conference proposal, 1956
Machine Learning (ML)+
A subset of AI in which a system improves its performance on a task by learning patterns from data, rather than following explicit hand-written rules.Source: Tom Mitchell, "Machine Learning," 1997 — standard CS textbook definition
Deep Learning+
A branch of ML that uses neural networks with many stacked layers ("deep") to learn increasingly complex patterns directly from raw data like text, images, or audio.Source: LeCun, Bengio & Hinton, "Deep Learning," Nature, 2015
Neural Network+
A model made of layers of connected "nodes" loosely inspired by neurons in the brain. Each connection has a weight that gets adjusted during training.Source: standard ML terminology, per Stanford CS231n / MIT OpenCourseWare
Large Language Model (LLM)+
A neural network trained on massive amounts of text to predict the next piece of text, which lets it read, write, summarize, and converse.Source: Anthropic and OpenAI model documentation
Foundation Model+
A large model trained on broad data that can be adapted (via prompting or fine-tuning) to many different downstream tasks, rather than being built for just one job.Source: Stanford Center for Research on Foundation Models (CRFM), 2021

⚙️ How models are built

Transformer+
The neural network architecture behind virtually all modern LLMs. It processes an entire sequence of text at once using "attention" instead of reading word-by-word like older models.Source: Vaswani et al., "Attention Is All You Need," Google, 2017
Attention mechanism+
The part of a transformer that lets the model weigh how relevant every other word in the input is to the word it's currently processing.Source: Vaswani et al., "Attention Is All You Need," Google, 2017
Parameter+
One of the internal numeric "knobs" a model adjusts during training. Model sizes are often described by parameter count (e.g., billions of parameters).Source: standard ML terminology, per Google Machine Learning Glossary
Training data+
The large collection of text (or images, audio, etc.) a model is shown so it can learn statistical patterns before it's ever used to answer questions.Source: standard ML terminology, per NIST AI Risk Management Framework, 2023
Pre-training+
The first, largest training stage, where a model learns general language patterns by predicting missing or next words across huge text datasets.Source: Radford et al., "Improving Language Understanding by Generative Pre-Training," OpenAI, 2018
Fine-tuning+
A second, smaller training stage that adapts a pre-trained model to a specific behavior or task using a more focused set of examples.Source: OpenAI and Anthropic developer documentation on fine-tuning
RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback)+
A training technique where humans rank different model responses, and the model is optimized to produce more of what people preferred.Source: Ouyang et al., "Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback" (InstructGPT), OpenAI, 2022
Constitutional AI+
A training method where a model critiques and revises its own responses against a written set of principles, reducing the amount of human labeling needed for safety training.Source: Bai et al., "Constitutional AI: Harmlessness from AI Feedback," Anthropic, 2022
Inference+
The act of running an already-trained model to produce an output — this is what happens every time you send it a message.Source: standard ML terminology, per NVIDIA and Anthropic technical documentation

💬 Working with a model

Token+
A chunk of text — often a whole word or part of one — that a model reads and generates. Text is converted to tokens before a model can process it.Source: Anthropic and OpenAI tokenizer documentation
Tokenization+
The process of splitting raw text into tokens before feeding it to a model, and converting generated tokens back into readable text.Source: Anthropic and OpenAI tokenizer documentation
PromptAB-730+
The text a person sends to a model — a question or instruction — that the model treats as the start of a pattern to continue.Source: standard industry terminology, per OpenAI/Anthropic API documentation
System prompt+
Instructions given to a model before the conversation starts, used to set its role, tone, or rules — separate from what the user types.Source: Anthropic and OpenAI API documentation
Prompt engineeringAB-730+
The practice of carefully wording instructions to a model to get more accurate or useful outputs.Source: Anthropic's "Prompt Engineering" developer documentation
Context window+
The amount of text (measured in tokens) a model can "see" at once — including the conversation so far and its own reply — before older content falls out of view.Source: Anthropic Claude Platform documentation, "Context windows"
Zero-shot / Few-shot+
Zero-shot means asking a model to do a task with no examples given. Few-shot means giving it a small handful of examples in the prompt first to guide its answer.Source: Brown et al., "Language Models are Few-Shot Learners" (GPT-3 paper), OpenAI, 2020
Chain-of-thought+
A prompting technique where a model is encouraged to write out intermediate reasoning steps before its final answer, often improving accuracy on multi-step problems.Source: Wei et al., "Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models," Google, 2022
Temperature+
A setting that controls how random or predictable a model's word choices are. Lower temperature gives more focused, repeatable answers; higher gives more varied, creative ones.Source: Anthropic and OpenAI API parameter documentation

🧭 Behavior, safety & limits

Hallucination+
When a model states false or fabricated information confidently, because it generated a plausible-sounding pattern rather than retrieving a verified fact.Source: widely used industry term; discussed in OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google model documentation
Alignment+
The effort to make a model's outputs match human intentions and values — being helpful, honest, and avoiding harmful behavior.Source: Anthropic, "Core Views on AI Safety," 2023
Guardrails+
Rules, filters, or training techniques applied to a model or the system around it to prevent unsafe, harmful, or off-policy outputs.Source: NIST AI Risk Management Framework, 2023
Bias (in AI)+
Systematic skew in a model's outputs that reflects imbalances or patterns present in its training data, which can lead to unfair or inaccurate results for certain groups.Source: NIST Special Publication 1270, "Towards a Standard for Identifying and Managing Bias in AI," 2022
Multimodal+
Describes a model that can take in and/or produce more than one type of data — for example, text and images together, not just text.Source: OpenAI (GPT-4), Google DeepMind (Gemini), and Anthropic (Claude) model documentation
Benchmark+
A standardized test or dataset used to measure and compare model performance on a specific skill, such as math, coding, or reading comprehension.Source: standard ML research terminology, e.g. MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2021)

🔬 Advanced techniques

Weights+
The actual numeric values a model's parameters hold after training — the learned "settings" that determine how input is transformed into output. Weights start out random and are adjusted throughout training.Source: Goodfellow, Bengio & Courville, "Deep Learning," MIT Press, 2016
Reinforcement Learning (RL)+
A training approach where a model learns by taking actions, receiving a reward or penalty, and adjusting to get more reward over time — trial, feedback, and repeat, rather than being shown labeled correct answers directly.Source: Sutton & Barto, "Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction," 2nd ed., MIT Press, 2018
Distillation+
A technique for training a smaller "student" model to mimic the outputs of a larger "teacher" model, producing a faster and cheaper model that keeps much of the original's capability.Source: Hinton, Vinyals & Dean, "Distilling the Knowledge in a Neural Network," 2015
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)+
A technique that retrieves relevant documents or data at the moment of a query and feeds them to the model alongside the prompt, grounding its answer in that specific content instead of relying only on what it learned during training.Source: Lewis et al., "Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks," Meta AI, 2020
Validation loss+
A score computed on held-out data (not used for training) that shows how well a model is generalizing during training. Lower is generally better, and a rising validation loss while training loss keeps falling is a classic sign of overfitting.Source: Google Machine Learning Crash Course, "Overfitting" module glossary
Coding agent+
An AI agent built specifically to write, run, test, and debug code with a degree of autonomy — going beyond simple autocomplete to carry out multi-step coding tasks with limited supervision.Source: Anthropic, Claude Code documentation; GitHub, Copilot documentation

🏛️ Classical AI & ML foundations

Symbolic AI+
An early approach to AI that represents knowledge as explicit symbols and rules, and reasons by manipulating those symbols logically — the dominant AI paradigm before machine learning became widespread.Source: Russell & Norvig, "Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach," 4th ed., 2020
Expert system+
A classical AI program that encodes a human expert's knowledge as if-then rules to make decisions or diagnoses in a narrow domain, such as early medical diagnosis programs like MYCIN.Source: Russell & Norvig, "Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach," 4th ed., 2020
Classification+
A machine learning task where a model assigns an input to one of a fixed set of categories, such as labeling an email as "spam" or "not spam."Source: Tom Mitchell, "Machine Learning," 1997
Regression+
A machine learning task where a model predicts a continuous numeric value, such as a house price or tomorrow's temperature, rather than a category.Source: Tom Mitchell, "Machine Learning," 1997
Supervised learning+
A machine learning approach where a model learns from data that's already labeled with the correct answer, then generalizes to label new, unseen data.Source: Tom Mitchell, "Machine Learning," 1997
Unsupervised learning+
A machine learning approach where a model finds patterns or groupings in data that has no labeled correct answers, such as clustering similar customers together.Source: Tom Mitchell, "Machine Learning," 1997

🧠 Neural network internals

Perceptron+
One of the earliest and simplest neural network units — it takes weighted inputs, sums them, and produces an output — forming the basic building block that modern neural networks scale up from.Source: Rosenblatt, "The Perceptron: A Probabilistic Model for Information Storage and Organization in the Brain," 1958
Cost function (loss function)+
A formula that measures how wrong a model's predictions are compared to the correct answers. Training works by adjusting weights to make this number as small as possible.Source: Goodfellow, Bengio & Courville, "Deep Learning," MIT Press, 2016
Backpropagation+
The algorithm used to train neural networks — it calculates how much each weight contributed to the error, then works backward through the network to adjust every weight accordingly.Source: Rumelhart, Hinton & Williams, "Learning representations by back-propagating errors," Nature, 1986
Activation function+
A mathematical function applied inside a neural network's units that introduces non-linearity, letting the network learn complex patterns instead of just straight-line relationships.Source: Goodfellow, Bengio & Courville, "Deep Learning," MIT Press, 2016
Hidden layer+
Any layer of a neural network between the input and output layers — where most of the network's actual pattern-learning happens. "Deep" learning refers to networks with many hidden layers.Source: Goodfellow, Bengio & Courville, "Deep Learning," MIT Press, 2016
Convolutional Neural Network (CNN)+
A neural network architecture especially effective at processing grid-like data such as images, using filters that scan across the input to detect visual patterns like edges and shapes.Source: LeCun et al., "Gradient-Based Learning Applied to Document Recognition," 1998
Recurrent Neural Network (RNN)+
A neural network architecture designed for sequential data (like text or time series), which loops information from previous steps back into the network — a precursor to the transformer architecture LLMs use today.Source: Rumelhart, Hinton & Williams, "Learning representations by back-propagating errors," Nature, 1986; Elman, "Finding Structure in Time," 1990
Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM)+
An improved type of recurrent neural network designed to remember information over longer sequences, addressing a key weakness of earlier RNNs — widely used before transformers became dominant.Source: Hochreiter & Schmidhuber, "Long Short-Term Memory," Neural Computation, 1997
Autoencoder+
A neural network trained to compress its input into a smaller representation and then reconstruct the original from that compressed form — used for tasks like compression, denoising, and anomaly detection.Source: Goodfellow, Bengio & Courville, "Deep Learning," MIT Press, 2016

🎨 Generative AI architectures

Diffusion model+
A generative model that learns to create content (often images) by starting from random noise and gradually "denoising" it step by step into a coherent result — the technique behind many image generators.Source: Ho, Jain & Abbeel, "Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models," 2020
Variational Autoencoder (VAE)+
A generative version of an autoencoder that learns a compressed, structured representation of data and can generate new, similar examples by sampling from that learned representation.Source: Kingma & Welling, "Auto-Encoding Variational Bayes," 2013

🕹️ Agentic AI

Agentic AI+
AI systems that go beyond replying to a single prompt — they can retain context, plan multi-step approaches, call external tools, and carry out tasks with a degree of autonomy before returning to the user.Source: Anthropic, "Building effective agents," 2024
Agent memory+
An agent's ability to retain information — from earlier in a task or across sessions — so it can act consistently instead of starting from scratch every step.Source: Anthropic, "Building effective agents," 2024
Planning (in agents)+
An agent's ability to break a larger goal into an ordered sequence of smaller steps before or while acting, rather than reacting to just one instruction at a time.Source: Anthropic, "Building effective agents," 2024
Tool use+
An agent's ability to call external functions, APIs, or software — like a search engine, calculator, or code interpreter — to take actions or retrieve information it couldn't produce from its own training alone.Source: Anthropic, "Tool use with Claude," developer documentation
Autonomous execution+
An agent carrying out a multi-step task on its own, checking its own progress and adjusting along the way, with limited step-by-step human approval required.Source: Anthropic, "Building effective agents," 2024

🏢 Ecosystem & access

API (Application Programming Interface)+
A way for developers to send requests to a model programmatically from their own software, rather than typing into a chat app.Source: standard software engineering terminology
Open-weight model+
A model whose trained parameters ("weights") are published publicly so anyone can download and run it on their own hardware.Source: Meta AI, "Llama" model license documentation
Closed / proprietary model+
A model that only runs on the provider's own servers and is accessed through an app or API, with the underlying weights kept private.Source: standard industry terminology, per Anthropic and OpenAI usage policies

Microsoft's low-code platform for building, extending, and managing conversational and autonomous AI agents, used to configure topics, flows, knowledge sources, and tool integrations.Source: Microsoft Copilot Studio documentation, learn.microsoft.com/microsoft-copilot-studio
Agent flowAB-620+
A defined sequence of steps (actions, connectors, logic) an agent runs to complete a task — the automation backbone that a topic can call into, similar in spirit to a workflow.Source: Microsoft Learn, AB-620 study guide — "Create an agent flow," "Add agent flows to a topic"
Human-in-the-loop (agent flow)AB-620+
A flow design pattern that pauses an agent's automated process to require a person's review or approval before a sensitive or high-stakes action completes.Source: Microsoft Learn, AB-620 study guide — "Create a human-in-the-loop agent flow"
Topic (Copilot Studio)AB-620+
A conversational building block in Copilot Studio that recognizes a specific user intent and defines how the agent should respond — through fixed logic, an agent flow, or a generative answer.Source: Microsoft Learn, AB-620 study guide — "Configure topics"
Generative answers nodeAB-620+
A component within a topic that lets the agent generate an AI response grounded in specified knowledge sources, rather than following a fixed, scripted reply.Source: Microsoft Learn, AB-620 study guide — "Configure generative answers node"
Adaptive cardAB-620+
A structured, interactive card format an agent can use to present information or collect input (buttons, forms, images) within a conversation, instead of plain text alone.Source: Microsoft Learn, AB-620 study guide — "Configure adaptive cards"
Model Context Protocol (MCP)AB-620+
An open protocol that gives an agent a standardized way to connect to and use external tools and data sources, rather than needing a custom integration built for each one.Source: Microsoft Learn, AB-620 study guide — Audience profile, "Configure MCP tools"
Agent2Agent (A2A) protocolAB-620+
An open protocol that lets separate AI agents communicate and collaborate directly with each other as part of a multi-agent solution, rather than only through a human intermediary.Source: Microsoft Learn, AB-620 study guide — "Create a multi-agent solution by using A2A protocol"
Computer use (agent tool)AB-620+
A tool capability that lets an agent interact with and automate tasks inside apps or websites the way a human would — clicking, typing, navigating — rather than only calling an API.Source: Microsoft Learn, AB-620 study guide — "Configure and monitor computer use for an agent"
Application Lifecycle Management (ALM) for agentsAB-620+
The discipline of packaging agents into Power Platform solutions and moving them consistently between environments (development, test, production) using environment variables and pipelines, rather than rebuilding by hand each time.Source: Microsoft Learn, AB-620 study guide — "Implement application lifecycle management (ALM) for agents in Copilot Studio"
Power Platform PipelinesAB-620+
A Microsoft tool for automating and standardizing how solutions — including agents — are deployed between environments (such as dev to test to production) in a controlled, repeatable way.Source: Microsoft Learn, AB-620 study guide — "Implement and extend Microsoft Power Platform Pipelines"

Closing the gap from "passing" to "confident"

An 88% on Microsoft's own practice assessment is a strong signal — you likely know the material. What tends to trip people up after that point isn't new content, it's scenario judgment: picking the right documented feature for a slightly unfamiliar business situation, not just recalling a definition.

Re-take the official practice assessment once more, cold

Don't review notes first. A clean second attempt shows you what you actually retain under pressure, not what you can recognize with prompting.

2

Drill the Final Scenario Review tier

It's deliberately framed as "what should this person do," not "what does this term mean" — closer to how a role-based exam actually tests judgment.

3

Sit the timed Full Exam Simulation

Mixed across all three domains with a running countdown clock, so you also practice pacing yourself — not just picking correct answers.

Run the exam sandbox once

Not for the content — for the interface. Knowing how interactive question types behave before test day removes one variable on exam day.
